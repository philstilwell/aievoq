The adoption gradient

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

AI adoption is the process of trying AI, finding useful roles for it and deciding how much work to entrust to it. People differ in experience, confidence, access and the results they achieve. Following those differences helps explain why the same technology can become a curiosity, a disappointment or a substantial extension of someone's abilities.

At one end of the clearing stands someone who has never used AI and is quite certain it cannot do anything valuable. At the other, someone has spent the morning building a tool, testing alternatives and finishing a project that would once have required outside help. The second person may have little appetite for another debate about whether AI can be useful. Between them lies much of the interesting terrain.

The useful question is what each person has actually tried, learned and achieved. A philosophical objection to calling machine output creative does not prevent someone from using it brilliantly. Enthusiasm does not establish that the work is better. A busy screen can conceal an unproductive afternoon.

Homo sapiens laborans is an imagined working human. The nine positions below describe patterns of behavior, not biological subspecies or a validated psychological scale. One person might be a workflow builder when preparing presentations, a cautious observer when analyzing finances and an informed abstainer when handling confidential material. Movement can go in either direction as tasks, tools and circumstances change.

1 / The categorical rejectors: the sealed burrow

Typical refrain: "There is nothing useful out there." They dismiss AI's practical or creative value without meaningful firsthand investigation. The burrow protects an established view from inconvenient encounters. The revealing question is whether any result could change their mind. This category concerns an untested blanket dismissal; considered objections belong elsewhere on the map.

2 / The disappointed experimenters: the abandoned feeding ground

"I tried it. It didn't work." They have experience, but a poor early encounter has become the verdict. Perhaps the tool failed, the task was unsuitable or the instructions lacked needed context. Returning with a different task or a newer tool might change the outcome. It might also confirm that this particular feeding ground offers little nourishment.

3 / The cautious observers: the forest edge

"Show me where it would help with my work." They recognize useful examples but hesitate over reliability, relevance or the effort of learning. They may need a demonstration involving their own problem, a manageable first experiment or permission from their employer. Their caution is a starting condition, not evidence that they cannot adapt.

4 / The curious explorers: exploratory foraging

"What else can this thing do?" They sample images, questions, games and unexpected combinations. Discovery is the reward; a dependable work habit has yet to form. This is fertile ground for finding new uses, although a folder full of delightful demonstrations does not tell us whether a recurring task became easier.

5 / The occasional adopters: opportunistic feeding

"This saves me a little time." They call on AI for an email, summary or brainstorming session, then return to familiar routines. The tool has a place without reorganizing the working day. Small, dependable gains can be entirely sufficient. There is no requirement that everyone turn an occasional convenience into an elaborate system.

6 / The selective specialists: a productive niche

"For this job, it earns its keep." They have found specific tasks where AI reliably helps. They know what information to supply, what to inspect and when to stop asking. Their advantage comes from knowing the boundaries of a useful niche. A narrow, well-tested application may deliver more value than indiscriminate use across an entire occupation.

7 / The workflow builders: habitat construction

"Why am I still doing these steps separately?" They rearrange the work around what AI makes possible: collecting material, drafting, comparing versions, checking and delivering results. Reusable instructions and connected tools reduce repetition. The relevant measurement includes the time spent constructing and maintaining this habitat, as well as the time saved once it works.

8 / The symbiotic practitioners: mutualism

"Together, we can tackle a harder problem." Humans provide direction, context and judgment; AI supplies speed, alternatives or assistance with unfamiliar tasks. The partnership deserves its name when its benefits survive checking and the person retains enough understanding to steer it. Dependence alone does not establish mutual benefit. Human-AI combinations must still be tested against the strongest available alternative. [78]

9 / The capacity multipliers: expansion into new niches

"I've already built the thing we're debating." They coordinate tools and repeatable processes to deliver substantially more useful work, or undertake projects previously beyond their practical reach. Some may claim gains of 100 times or more. That is a report to investigate, not the admission requirement for this category. The strongest evidence is a sustained record of accepted results, with costs and checking included. Being too busy to argue is no substitute for that record.

Three paths that escape the ladder

Uncritical enthusiasts may use AI constantly while verifying little. They resemble vigorous growth with weak roots: output expands, but its value remains uncertain. This pattern can occur at several positions, including among people who have built elaborate workflows.

Informed abstainers understand or have tested the tools and decide against a particular use. Privacy, poor performance, creative preference or an unacceptable cost can make abstention sensible. They should not be placed in the sealed burrow merely because their conclusion is unfavorable to AI.

Constrained would-be users want to experiment but lack access, training, suitable equipment or permission. A closed gate is different from a refusal to enter. Opportunities to adopt also depend on employers, institutions and the distribution of resources; the framework does not make displaced workers responsible for their own job losses.

Confidence is not a stopwatch

Two independent studies make the distinction concrete. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied 5,172 customer-support agents and found a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour with AI access. Gains varied substantially across workers. This is evidence of useful assistance in a particular workplace. [46]

In Becker and colleagues' randomized study, 16 experienced developers handled 246 tasks in familiar open-source projects using tools available in early 2025. AI access increased completion time by 19%. Participants nevertheless estimated afterward that it had reduced their time by 20%. The preprint concerns that setting and those tools; it does not settle the performance of later systems. [97]

These results measure different things in different populations. They cannot be combined into a universal AI productivity score. They do show why experience, task fit and measured outcomes deserve separate entries in the field notebook. Neither study counted the nine categories proposed here.

The 100-times field test

An extraordinary gain could involve accelerating one small step, increasing the volume of finished work or making an entirely new kind of project feasible. Those are different achievements. A useful investigation asks:

What is the unit? Drafts generated, problems solved, accepted designs and completed projects are different outputs. Define an accepted result before comparing it.

What is the baseline? Compare similar tasks, the same worker or comparable workers, and a credible earlier method. An artificially awkward baseline can manufacture a spectacular multiplier.

What does the clock include? Count preparation, instructions, review, corrections and recurring maintenance. Report initial setup separately and explain how many uses share that cost. Distinguish human working time from elapsed waiting time.

Did quality survive? Apply the same acceptance standard to both methods, ideally without telling evaluators which method produced which result. Record failures and discarded work as well as successes.

What else was consumed? Record software and computing costs, outside help and any remaining human bottleneck. Compare repeated attempts, not only the best demonstration.

For comparable tasks, useful output per human work-hour supplies one defensible productivity ratio. It should appear alongside quality, total cost and elapsed time. When someone could not previously undertake the task at all, describe the newly accessible capability; a zero baseline cannot yield a meaningful finite multiplier.

The quantum echo: try it, check it

There is an appealing parallel with the practical disposition often summarized as "Shut up and calculate." We can exploit a tool's usefulness while still arguing about its interpretation. Someone need not settle whether an AI truly understands before testing whether it helps produce a sound result.

The historical label needs care. N. David Mermin used the phrase in a 1989 column and revisited its attribution in 2004, objecting to its unsubstantiated association with Richard Feynman. Mermin also regretted its dismissiveness. It should not be presented as an uncomplicated founding motto of early quantum theory. [98]

For AI, the more useful disposition is "Try it. Check it. Then decide what it makes possible." Questions about understanding, ownership, safety and social consequences remain legitimate. Practical results and serious criticism can improve one another.

What can be followed from quarter to quarter?

The October 22 Census release supplies a current anchor: 24.6% of U.S. employer businesses reported recent AI use, up from a 23.8% Q3 reference snapshot. The latest reporting window spans September 21-October 4; the 0.8 percentage-point difference is neither a completed Q4 result nor a measure of these human categories. The adoption census explains the consistent question and historical reconstruction. [86], [92]

Alongside that established series, a proposed recurring reader field notebook would follow specific tasks: first trial, continued use, independently checked output, human minutes and total cost. Revisit willing participants using the same questions and report how many return. A voluntary reader sample describes its respondents, not the population.

The nine positions can guide interviews and help readers recognize their own habits. Their population shares, movement rates and typical productivity multipliers are not yet measured. The enduring story is the interaction between a changing habitat and the human choices, opportunities and skills within it.
