The naturalist's scorecard

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

Forecast accuracy compares an earlier prediction with what actually happened. A hit rate counts correct directional calls; probability scoring also asks how confident the forecast was. A fair record keeps misses, unresolved cases and the original wording visible.

There is a special pleasure in discovering that the animal confidently identified as a rare new species was the familiar one wearing mud. A forecast department should have room for that embarrassment. The useful question is what the mistake teaches us about the habitat.

The recurring results panel

MeasureWhat readers will see
Forecasts resolvedThe number judged yes or no, with separate counts for pending and unresolvable cases.
Hits and missesCorrect and incorrect calls, with the original prediction beside the observed result.
Hit rateCorrect directional calls divided by all resolved directional calls, always with the count: for example, 7 of 10, not a percentage alone.
Probability errorMean binary Brier error, from 0 (best) to 1 (worst), with the number of forecasts scored. Lower is better.
Confidence checkOnce enough results accumulate, compare stated probabilities with how often those events occurred. Show the number of observations and uncertainty.
What changed our mindsA short explanation of the miss, an overlooked pressure or a better measure, followed by a newly dated outlook.

A 70% prediction is a belief that an event is fairly likely, with a meaningful chance of failure. Over many comparable predictions assigned 70%, we would hope to see roughly seven in ten occur. A few cases cannot establish whether confidence is well judged. Forecast-scoring research supplies methods for testing probabilities; it does not validate the probabilities in our draft. [126], [127]

Illustration, not a result: a 70% forecast receives a binary Brier error of 0.09 if the event happens and 0.49 if it does not. This penalizes confident mistakes more heavily. The calculation is the squared difference between the probability and the outcome, recorded as 1 for yes or 0 for no. Average those errors across eligible resolved forecasts. We use the one-event binary convention, whose 0–1 range is half the two-category sum in Brier's original formulation. A constant 50% guess scores 0.25, a simple reference rather than an expert benchmark. [126], [127]

No moving the finish line

Keep each original locked claim, probability, source cutoff and test. Record the observed value or event, evidence link, review date, verdict and explanation. Count each locked forecast once with equal weight; do not improve the record by dropping a miss, adding easy retrospective calls or repeatedly counting the same success. Show withdrawn drafts and unresolvable cases with reasons.

A hit means the more likely outcome occurred: above 50% predicts yes, below 50% predicts no. Exactly 50% makes no directional call, so it contributes to probability error but not the hit-rate denominator. Pending and unresolvable cases contribute to neither. Never label one minus the Brier error as an accuracy percentage.

Compare like cohorts: quarterly with quarterly, annual with annual, and related topics where useful. Different event difficulty and subject matter can change an average without any improvement in forecasting skill. Preserve corrections openly. If a prediction comes true only after its deadline, report the later development while retaining the original miss.

The recurring layout will pair what we expected, what happened, and what we learned. There are no invented outcome rows to fill the space this quarter. The first results will appear when the original envelopes can fairly be opened.
