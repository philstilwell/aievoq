Fork it: the right for purists to branch

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

AI coding tools can propose changes and inspect software for mistakes. The controversy concerns how much to trust their output, who must check it, and whether objections should govern one person's choices or an entire project's rules. These questions shape how a working community adopts AI.

Suspicion, dissent and the price of trust

Dates: July 14-15, 2026 dispute / Q3 background. October 8-14 developments / Q4 update. Reviewed October 30.

Who checks the new helper?

The Linux argument began with a question that deserves a serious answer: should an AI reviewer send its findings straight to the programmer whose work it examined? Developer Laurent Pinchart wanted a human maintainer to check those reports first. Sashiko developer Roman Gushchin argued that requiring this extra relay would undermine the system's purpose of saving maintainers work. Both positions concern a scarce resource: experienced human attention. [106]

Imagine a new helper arriving at the penguin colony, offering to inspect every nest. The residents want to know whether it spots real weaknesses, whether its warnings can be trusted, and who must investigate them. Faster inspection is attractive. A flood of dubious warnings would be another burden.

Several different objections can hide inside the phrase anti-AI. Practical skepticism asks for evidence of reliability and net benefit. A principled refusal concerns the methods someone is willing to use. Categorical dismissal rules out useful results before examining them. These are editorial distinctions, not diagnoses of the people in this dispute. Treating every critic as the same kind of purist makes the argument less informative.

When suspicion protects, and when it hardens

The biological analogy is a colony's alarm system: sensitivity can protect the nests, but indiscriminate alarm can obstruct useful cooperation. The question is how well the response distinguishes a real threat from an unfamiliar arrival.

Research suggests that trust can tilt in either direction. In five studies reported in 2015, Dietvorst and colleagues found that people could lose confidence in forecasting algorithms after seeing mistakes, even when the algorithms performed better than human alternatives. They called this algorithm aversion. [113]

Logg and colleagues' 2019 research found algorithm appreciation: in several estimation and forecasting tasks, people gave more weight to advice they believed came from an algorithm. The effect weakened when people compared it with their own judgment or had forecasting expertise. Both papers predate today's coding assistants; neither measures Linux developers' attitudes or proves that a particular objection is misplaced. Together, they argue against a simple story in which humans always distrust machines. [114]

The useful test is whether a position can respond to evidence. A skeptic can specify what would make a tool acceptable. An enthusiast should be equally willing to specify what would make it unacceptable.

Torvalds draws the line

Linus Torvalds, Linux's creator and chief maintainer, treated AI as a useful tool whose merits should be judged in practice. His July response acknowledged that it could create painful workloads for maintainers. He wanted those problems addressed so that the tools helped the project. Personal abstention remained allowed; he rejected attempts to turn it into a prohibition on other contributors' use. [105]

That position leaves substantial room for dissent about particular tools, tasks and submissions. The kernel's guidance keeps a human responsible for reviewing generated work. A contributor may have to explain it, test it and defend it. A machine's fluent output does not relieve its submitter of that responsibility. [107]

At the October 8 Maintainers Summit, the practical concern was again the quality of contributions and the capacity to review them. Corbet's October 12 account describes support for capable AI users alongside concern about weak submissions and the treatment of newcomers. The colony still needs residents who can maintain what they bring home. [108]

The panel counts disclosed assistance, not defects prevented or hours saved. On October 14, the kernel removed the mandatory Assisted-by label because inconsistent reporting made its absence uninformative. That breaks a simple adoption trend; it does not settle whether the tools are helping. The more revealing measures would be accepted improvements, later faults and total reviewer effort. [109]

A right to disagree, and a cost to refusing

Blanket refusal may become costly where assisted rivals demonstrate better results after checking and repair are counted. Deliberate AI-free practices may retain a valued niche. Those are conditional expectations about working methods, not an expiry date for skeptics. Dissent that identifies a real weakness can improve the whole community.

Torvalds' blunt exit clause was that opponents "can do the open-source thing and fork it": take the shared code and develop an independent project. [105] The central dispute remains closer to home: which suspicions improve the work, and which prevent useful tools from receiving a fair trial?
