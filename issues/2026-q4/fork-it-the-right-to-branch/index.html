Fork it: the right to branch

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

AI-assisted software development uses AI to suggest, inspect or revise code while people decide what to accept and take responsibility for the result. In open-source projects, a fork is a separate development effort built from shared code. Disagreement over AI can therefore change both how a community works and whether its members continue working together.

A dispute at the nest entrance

The argument began with a practical question: should an automated reviewer send its findings directly to the person whose code it examined? Sashiko, an AI review system, was finding problems in proposed Linux changes. Developer Laurent Pinchart wanted maintainers, the people who review and look after the code, to check its reports before passing them to authors. Sashiko developer Roman Gushchin argued that this extra human relay would defeat the aim of saving maintainers work. That is a dispute about who bears the checking burden, not simply whether machines can do anything useful. [106]

Linus Torvalds, Linux's creator and chief maintainer of its kernel, the core that coordinates software and hardware, then drew a boundary. In a July 14 message, dated July 15 in UTC, he rejected a project-wide anti-AI position. Those who objected, he wrote, "can do the open-source thing and fork it." He also said developers were not being forced to use AI. His position allowed personal abstention while refusing to let it become a ban on other contributors' tools. [105]

The penguin colony could accommodate different fishing techniques. Anyone demanding a colony-wide prohibition had the option of establishing a different colony.

A fork is a possibility, not a verdict

Forking means starting with a copy of the software and developing it independently, under the applicable licence. It offers a practical route for disagreement: build the alternative, attract collaborators and see whether people find it worth using. Keeping it useful requires more than copying files. Someone must review changes, repair faults, prepare releases and sustain the community. Copying the nest is easier than keeping the chicks alive. [110]

The biological parallel is a shared lineage dividing into populations that experience different selection pressures. An AI-permitting project might emphasize assisted experimentation and review; an AI-restricting project might emphasize other priorities, such as a particular assurance process or working culture. These are possible niches, not measured advantages. Each would still have to satisfy its users.

This is also where the metaphor needs room to bend. Software communities can deliberately choose rules, exchange improvements and reunite. A copied project does not instantly become a new species. A durable fork would need its own continuing development, meaningful differences and a reason for users to stay.

October: the selection pressure moves to review

At the October 8 Maintainers Summit, participants debated the growing review burden. Corbet's October 12 firsthand account describes Torvalds favoring capable users of AI while urging low priority for weak contributions. Others stressed the need to retain promising newcomers. The difficult distinction was between useful assistance and submissions whose authors could not sustain a meaningful technical conversation. [108]

The published guidance supplies the practical boundary: a human remains responsible for reviewing AI-generated code and for the submission. Maintainers can demand explanation or testing, apply extra scrutiny, or reject work the submitter cannot defend. Permission to use AI does not guarantee acceptance of its output. [107]

In ecological terms, producing more offspring does not create more food. Producing more proposed changes does not create more reviewer attention. If AI lowers the cost of generating code, the scarce resource may become the experienced human able to decide which changes deserve to live. That is an interpretation of the bottleneck, not a measured productivity loss.

When the census loses its identifying tag

The October report counted 1,884 Assisted-by labels among 18,533 individual code changes in Linux 7.3 through its seventh preview release. That count excludes records that combine development branches. Their ratio is 10.2%. It measures recorded labels, not the true prevalence of AI assistance. [108]

On October 14, a recorded change to the kernel's documentation removed the mandatory label. Its explanation says inconsistent disclosure made absence of the tag uninformative. Expectations of transparency about tool use and human responsibility remained. [109]

The naturalist has lost a particular identifying band, not discovered that the birds have disappeared. Future tag counts cannot simply continue the old adoption line. A decline could reflect the changed recording rule. More informative tracking would distinguish accepted improvements, faults introduced, reviewer time and contributors who remain to maintain their work.

The research beneath the branches

This analogy has a software-research foundation. Robles and Gonzalez-Barahona's 2012 study identified 220 significant forks and examined why they formed and what became of them. It documents varied paths, including continued separate development and discontinuation. It does not provide survival odds for a hypothetical AI-free Linux. [110]

Wang and colleagues' 2023 paper went further toward the ecological vocabulary. Using 50 GitHub projects, it drew on biodiversity research to measure how different a project's forks were, beyond simply counting them. Its results concern associations in those projects, not proof that more branching causes better software. Its platform-level forks and the older study's significant project splits are different units. [111]

The research supports watching what branches actually do. An announcement, a download or a repository copy is a poor substitute for a living development community.

Where this belongs on the adoption gradient

Torvalds' position fits a practical partnership: use the tool, understand the work and remain answerable for it. It does not establish a 100-times gain, nor does it turn every critic into a categorical rejector. Pinchart's concern about unchecked reviews belongs beside the questions asked by selective specialists and informed abstainers. The same person can welcome AI in one part of a workflow and resist it in another.

The revealing question is whether an objection can be tested against outcomes. Does a review rule improve accepted work or merely move effort onto someone else? Does an AI restriction protect a valuable practice, or close off a useful adaptation? Neither answer follows from the loudness of the dispute.

Speciation watch / open: the July statement offered a route to a separate project. The reviewed evidence does not establish a sustained anti-AI Linux fork arising from it. Record a new lineage only when its independent development and distinguishing practices can be observed. For now, the fork is an available branch in the tree, not an animal already caught in the field guide.
