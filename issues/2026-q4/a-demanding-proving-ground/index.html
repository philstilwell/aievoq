A demanding proving ground

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

A benchmark is a defined set of tasks used to test an AI system. Comparisons are useful when the tasks, tools, time and scoring stay consistent. A specialized mathematics benchmark measures performance on those problems, not intelligence in every setting.

Some familiar tests have become too easy to reveal much. A useful recurring score needs a clearly defined challenge and enough difficulty to leave room for change. Keep separate tracks for different kinds of ability; no single test measures the whole ecosystem. [8], [40]

Epoch AI's FrontierMath Erdős asks systems to prove or disprove 68 conjectures, with one attempt each, at most $300 and 72 hours per attempt. The checking software must accept the proof. In the October 28 export, the highest reported score is 3 of 68, from GPT-6.1 Sol at its maximum reasoning setting. [90], [91]

A baseline, not a victory lap. The leading point estimates differ by very few solved items and their uncertainty overlaps. These results do not establish a reliable ordering of general mathematical ability. Eight other tested configurations recorded zero successes in this export; zero observed successes is not proof of zero capability.

The future comparison must keep the challenge fixed. Changes to the problem set, tools, budget or scoring require a new series, or retesting on a common basis. Published solutions may later enter training data; any exclusion of those problems changes the denominator. Model release dates also differ from the dates tests become public. [90]

The older METR time-horizon chart remains valuable background, but its page is no longer maintained. A frozen instrument belongs in the archive, with its last update visible. [10]
