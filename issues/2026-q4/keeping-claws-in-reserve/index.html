Keeping claws in reserve

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

Delegating work to AI changes which skills people exercise. A delegated skill may remain available, weaken through disuse or be replaced by another method. The practical question is whether people can still judge the output and recover when the assistant is unavailable.

Our gain-of-function pages follow what AI acquires. Alongside them, we can follow what people stop doing, what they still understand, and what they may need to recover. Delegation is not automatically a loss: the ability may remain intact, or a better way of working may replace it.

Judgment needs practice too. Asking someone to approve an answer is not proof that they can assess it. The learning and automation studies give us reasons to test that capacity, rather than assume a human signature supplies it. [44], [45], [56], [57] Nor do they show that beginners and experienced experts suddenly start from the same position.

For each workplace encounter, this proposed ledger would distinguish three choices. They are editorial questions, not a prescription to preserve every old skill.

Treatment of a skillWhy keep or release it?What could we observe?
Keep in regular useNeeded to understand the task, detect consequential errors, or choose worthwhile goals.Explain a decision; check an unfamiliar example with planted mistakes.
Rehearse as a reserveNeeded occasionally when a system fails or conditions change.Try a bounded exercise without the usual assistant; record help and recovery time.
Delegate with a fallbackKeeping full proficiency may cost more than it contributes in this setting.Document the alternative person, tool or procedure, and test whether it works.

Proposed encounter: two drafts and a diagnosis. On a safe practice task, have a learner attempt an answer before seeing AI's version. Compare both with an independently checked reference. A mentor explains the differences; the learner then tries a fresh problem. We would report accuracy, understanding and time separately. This exercise is a design to test, not a proven training recipe.

A new survival question: does the partnership replenish the expertise it needs? A department can finish more work this quarter while its next generation learns less. It can also use AI to teach better. Our report should look for either outcome, and revisit the balance whenever the tools or the work change.

The future need not be a ladder on which humans endlessly retreat toward "strategy." Checking, choosing goals and teaching can also change. The interesting story is which useful abilities persist, where they reside, and who can still exercise them.
