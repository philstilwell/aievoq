Fewer footholds for the young

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

Early-career employment is one place to look for changes in entry into paid work. Comparing younger workers across occupations with different AI exposure can identify an emerging pattern. The comparison remains an employment indicator, not a direct count of dismissals caused by AI.

A nursery can become harder to enter while the adults remain busy. For Homo sapiens laborans, the first sign of pressure may be a narrower path into paid work.

ADP and Stanford's October release records a contrast: employment in highly AI-exposed occupations fell while the least-exposed group grew. The gap was larger among workers aged 22-25. These are September-to-September changes released during Q4, not jobs lost during October. [79]

Read the tracks carefully. Exposure describes tasks an AI might affect; it does not establish that an employer used AI. Employment counts reflect hiring and departures together. This chart cannot tell how much of the change came from layoffs, reduced recruitment or other economic forces. [79], [80]

A different pool, a different current

In an October 6 research dispatch, Humlum and Vestergaard revisited surveys of 25,000 Danish workers in 11 occupations, linked to records through December 2024. They found no detectable average effect on earnings or hours, despite changes in tasks. This is an October discussion of earlier research, with an earlier observation window. [81]

The insight: a changing task, a shrinking occupation and a lost job are three different sightings. The U.S. payroll pattern deserves attention; the Danish comparison explains why exposure alone cannot identify AI's contribution. Different places, periods and questions can yield different answers.
