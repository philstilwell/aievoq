Expectations & forecasts / 2 min read

The Naturalist Was Wrong

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

An AI result challenges an expectation only if we know what was expected and what the new evidence establishes. Dated predictions, unstated assumptions and claims of surprise need different treatment. Comparing them carefully shows which beliefs actually need revision.

October's endangered expectations

The naturalist arrives carrying a confident field guide. By lunchtime, the bird has borrowed a screwdriver, the octopus has hired assistants, and the spider has submitted a paper. The field guide requires an appendix.

A supplied transcript tells a lively story: predictions of a ceiling for large language models gave way to new routes to improvement. [26] It invites us to revisit expectations. But it also bundles the skeptics together, bundles the optimists together, and awards the jungle to the winner.

Past / Keep the original prediction in view

A warning that training data may become scarce is different from a prediction that all AI progress will stop. Epoch's 2024 study projected possible full use of public human text between 2026 and 2032 under specified trends; it also considered other sources of progress. October's advances do not, by themselves, refute that forecast. [32]

Nor was everybody surprised. In a February 2026 interview, Dario Amodei described the broad progress of models as roughly consistent with his earlier expectations, while acknowledging unevenness. That is one person's account, but enough to complicate the transcript's opening chorus. [33]
