Demand & resources / 2 min read

When the feeding grounds fill

By Phil Stilwell · Reviewed October 30, 2026 · Q4 in progress

AI concept

Meeting AI demand requires computing capacity, electricity, investment and human work. A shortage in one can raise costs or limit access even if models improve. Demand and available supply must therefore be examined together, using a separate measure for each resource.

People want useful results. Delivering those results calls on electricity, equipment, money and human attention. These are connected appetites, but counting one does not measure all the others.

The physical appetite is already substantial. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres worldwide used about 485 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025, and projects roughly 950 in 2030 in its central outlook. The latter is a projection. Both figures cover all data centres, including non-AI activity; they are not measurements of AI alone. [74]

A cheaper mouthful need not mean a smaller total meal. If serving each task takes less energy but many more tasks are attempted, total use can still rise. Research measuring AI's operating energy finds large differences across tasks and models; the energy outlook also considers changing efficiency and workload. There is no single enduring electricity price tag for an "AI question." [74], [75]

The bottleneck can move. More chips do not immediately provide a power connection, a dependable answer or a colleague who knows when to question it. Energy analysis and research on organizational investment describe different constraints on expansion. Removing one can make another more noticeable. [73], [74]

The ecological analogy is carrying capacity: the activity that available resources can support. For AI, infrastructure, efficiency, prices and human choices can change the limits. The comparison does not imply a fixed number of AI creatures that the planet can hold.

The implications reach outside the enclosure. Growing demand could widen access to useful assistance while increasing pressure on local electricity systems. Scarcity could favor well-funded providers; it could also reward economical specialists. These are possibilities to investigate. A full order book alone does not show which species will thrive, who will benefit, or who will bear the costs.
