# Welcome to the AI jungle

[CONCEPT: AI systems use trained models to produce answers or choose actions. Their usefulness also depends on tools, access, price and human decisions. Following AI therefore means tracking both what the technology can do and the conditions that let people use it.]

## Every jungle has an electricity bill

[PLATE:living-jungle:78]

Imagine arriving in a jungle where the inhabitants are redesigned every few weeks, the food supply is metered, and the keepers sometimes remove an animal because its younger cousin is cheaper to feed. The animals have release notes. The weather has subscription tiers.

This journal follows the creatures through a changing season. Some acquire useful new tricks. Some find a neglected niche. Others discover that yesterday's impressive performance has become today's ordinary expectation. Around them, people rearrange the habitat: connecting tools, changing prices, granting access, or taking it away.

Our first issue follows a particularly consequential migration: AI into the production of mathematical research. The resulting upheaval involves much more than difficult puzzles. It touches the way people discover, check, understand, and share knowledge. Those questions will travel with AI into other fields.

Three invented creatures will guide us. **The Ledgerfinch** stands for economical, repeated information work. **The Relay Octopus** represents an assistant reaching into tools and outside services. **The Proofweaver** accompanies the story of mathematical discovery. **Homo sapiens laborans**, the working human, joins them to explore displacement and partnership.

The creatures make functions memorable; the statistics let us test the story. A recurring census follows adoption, employment, capability, operating prices and energy. Each measure keeps its population, date and comparison visible. A new feather is an illustration; a measured improvement needs evidence.

## A few things to keep in the field bag

A model is the underlying AI. A working system adds tools, information, and permissions. A product packages these for people to use. Their fortunes can differ: a product may gain a function without its model changing, or a model may disappear from one product while remaining available elsewhere.

Broad conclusions need more than one source. We draw on research papers, show disagreement, and distinguish findings from our own ecological metaphors. Company claims stay attributed. The longer source trail is at the back, so the story can keep moving.

**The question for this quarter:** which creatures are becoming more useful, and how are their successes changing the world around them?

# Why this is an evolutionary story

[CONCEPT: AI development creates competing designs, reuses earlier methods and retains some approaches while abandoning others. These processes resemble variation, inheritance and selection. The comparison includes deliberate human design; it does not mean that AI models are living organisms.]

## The case for a jungle with engineers

[PLATE:living-jungle:86]

The birds, octopuses and spiders earn their place in this journal through a serious resemblance. AI development produces variants, carries earlier achievements into later designs, and gives some approaches more opportunities to continue. The surrounding world helps decide what spreads. That pattern makes evolutionary thinking useful well beyond a picturesque vocabulary.

**The core connection is variation, inheritance and selection.** Biological populations contain differences; some differences are passed on; and those differences can affect the contribution organisms make to later generations. These are central conditions for evolution by natural selection. Research on cultural evolution asks how related processes operate when people transmit and modify knowledge and technology. [60], [61]

In AI, inheritance has several routes. A new version can retain an earlier model's learned settings, or a new model can learn from another model's responses. Researchers also transmit methods and combine existing components. These routes differ, but each can carry something forward instead of beginning again from nothing. [64]-[66]

**Sometimes the resemblance is an implemented mechanism.** One research method varies and selects neural networks, allowing their structures to change across generations. Another, Population Based Training, lets stronger performers supply copied settings to other members of a training population, followed by further variation. These are concrete examples from separate research teams, not evidence that every AI is trained this way. [62], [63]

**People belong inside the explanation.** Engineers deliberately invent variants; users and institutions help determine which are retained. Intentional design changes the process, but does not erase copying, variation or selective retention. Cultural-evolution research and a computer model of technological recombination provide a bridge between engineering and evolutionary analysis. [61], [66]

Our claim is therefore specific: **many AI development processes share evolutionary mechanisms, while the wider AI world also exhibits ecological relationships worth investigating.** A company rivalry is not automatically natural selection. We must identify what varies, what is transmitted and what makes a lineage persist. The metaphor earns its keep when it helps us ask those questions more precisely.

# Common dynamics / 1

[CONCEPT: AI systems differ in their capabilities and costs. Developers reuse successful components, while users and institutions influence which designs spread. These are distinct processes: producing a variation, carrying something forward and favoring one option over another.]

## What changes, what is inherited, what spreads

These are comparisons of processes. The papers establish particular mechanisms and examples; the cross-domain map is our synthesis.

[PLATE:possible-branches:60]

| Shared dynamic | In living systems | In AI development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Variation** | Individuals differ; new genetic combinations and mutations supply further differences. | Changed training, learned settings and system designs create alternatives. Some methods explicitly generate populations of variants. [60], [62], [63] |
| **2. Inheritance** | Descendants retain features of earlier generations, allowing changes to accumulate. | Earlier model settings can be retained; teaching from another model offers a different transmission route. [60], [64], [65] |
| **3. Selection** | Heritable differences can affect representation in later generations. | Training procedures can preferentially copy better performers. Across products, selective retention needs evidence of reuse and continued development. [60], [62], [63] |
| **4. Local adaptation** | An advantage depends on the habitat; different conditions can favor different specialists. | Task-specific training and evaluation make usefulness conditional on the job and setting. There need not be one universal winner. [60], [64], [40] |
| **5. Constraints and trade-offs** | Useful functions can be costly to maintain; dependence can sometimes reduce that burden. | Accuracy, speed, computing cost and human checking can pull in different directions. Better designs can also improve several together. [54], [55], [36], [40] |
| **6. Recombination and transfer** | Inherited material can be reshuffled; evolution can work with existing components. | Network designs, model-derived knowledge and tools can be combined or transferred. Combining working parts does not guarantee a working whole. [62], [65], [66] |

**The reporting test:** identify the variant, the thing carried forward, and the reason it was retained. A rising score alone does not establish all three.

# Common dynamics / 2

[CONCEPT: AI development also involves combining methods, finding new uses for existing abilities and relying on other systems. Outcomes depend on those relationships and on earlier decisions. A model's own capabilities explain only part of its success or failure.]

## How creatures change one another's prospects

Some parallels concern evolution across generations; others concern ecological relationships. We will name which process a story actually shows.

[PLATE:habitat-engineers:54]

| Shared dynamic | In living systems | In AI development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **7. Repurposing** | An existing feature can acquire a new use: biologists call this exaptation. | Capabilities developed in one setting can be recruited for another. We should trace the original role and the later use. [64], [68] |
| **8. Cooperation and dependence** | Partners can gain from exchanging services, while becoming vulnerable to their loss. | Models, tools and people can form useful partnerships. Their connections bring both reach and dependencies. [54], [55], [42], [43] |
| **9. Reciprocal adaptation** | A change in one population can alter pressures on another, inviting a response. | Opposing agents in a hide-and-seek experiment developed successive counter-strategies. Industry-wide arms races need their own evidence. [59], [67] |
| **10. Habitat construction** | Organisms alter surroundings and sometimes the conditions shaping future selection. | AI use can change workflows and the skills people practice, changing what becomes useful next. That feedback is a reporting question. [58], [59], [44], [46] |
| **11. Branching and specialization** | Related populations can diverge; some divergences eventually produce distinct species. | A shared model can support distinct adaptations. Durable differences in role may justify our provisional species portraits. [62], [64], [69] |
| **12. Historical contingency** | Earlier changes can make later innovations possible; another history might take another route. | Available components and inherited designs can shape what is feasible next. Present arrangements need not be inevitable or optimal. [66], [69] |

**Two further clues:** similar functions can arise along different paths, suggesting convergence; disappearance from a habitat can suggest replacement. Both require a history. Similarity does not prove independent origin, and an unavailable product may leave reusable descendants behind.

# Where the analogy earns its keep

[CONCEPT: An AI system can change in several ways: its answer can change during a conversation, its model can receive further training, or its tools and access can change. Distinguishing these levels lets us identify what actually improved and what evidence would demonstrate it.]

## Four distinctions that make the jungle more revealing

[PLATE:naturalist-surprised:84]

**A lifetime is different from a lineage.** An assistant changing its answer after feedback shows adjustment during use. Further training changes a model. Evolutionary change concerns what is retained and transmitted across a population or succession of designs. These can interact, as Population Based Training illustrates, but they should not be counted as the same event. [60], [63]

**A market is a habitat with decision-makers.** Biological evolution has no foresight or promised destination. AI developers can pursue explicit goals, while funding, distribution, rules and user choices shape which designs continue. We must investigate those choices rather than attribute every winner to technical superiority. Cultural evolution supplies a framework for examining human transmission; it does not make commercial outcomes inevitable. [61], [66]

**A model, a system and a company have different family histories.** Reusing learned settings, learning from another model and borrowing a research idea are different relationships. Connecting a calculator adds a component to a system. It does not, by itself, show genetic-style inheritance. Our species are functional characters; actual ancestry needs a documented development trail. [64], [65], [42], [43]

**Fitness is not a moral verdict or a universal intelligence score.** In biology it concerns contribution to future generations in a setting. For AI, continued use, copying and investment are possible measures of persistence; reliable, economical work is a separate outcome. We should measure both before explaining either. Growth can reward a feature we would prefer to discourage. [60], [40]

Chance also belongs in the story. Biological evolution includes random changes in the frequency of inherited variants, called genetic drift. In AI, chance discoveries or copying fashions may matter, but the label is only useful if we can identify the mechanism. The bacterial experiment in [69] illustrates a related lesson: earlier changes can affect which innovations become possible.

**What the analogy buys us:** follow inheritance as well as novelty; dependencies as well as abilities; and changing surroundings as well as benchmark scores. It makes no automatic prediction of consciousness, independent reproduction, endless improvement or a single species taking over the forest.

# A demanding proving ground

[CONCEPT: A benchmark is a defined set of tasks used to test an AI system. Comparisons are useful when the tasks, tools, time and scoring stay consistent. A specialized mathematics benchmark measures performance on those problems, not intelligence in every setting.]

## Capability / the same challenge, the same budget

[STATS:capability]

Some familiar tests have become too easy to reveal much. A useful recurring score needs a clearly defined challenge and enough difficulty to leave room for change. Keep separate tracks for different kinds of ability; no single test measures the whole ecosystem. [8], [40]

[FIGURE:capability_baseline]

Epoch AI's **FrontierMath Erdős** asks systems to prove or disprove **68 conjectures**, with one attempt each, at most **$300 and 72 hours per attempt**. The checking software must accept the proof. In the October 28 export, the highest reported score is **3 of 68**, from GPT-6.1 Sol at its maximum reasoning setting. [90], [91]

**A baseline, not a victory lap.** The leading point estimates differ by very few solved items and their uncertainty overlaps. These results do not establish a reliable ordering of general mathematical ability. Eight other tested configurations recorded zero successes in this export; zero observed successes is not proof of zero capability.

**The future comparison must keep the challenge fixed.** Changes to the problem set, tools, budget or scoring require a new series, or retesting on a common basis. Published solutions may later enter training data; any exclusion of those problems changes the denominator. Model release dates also differ from the dates tests become public. [90]

The older METR time-horizon chart remains valuable background, but its page is no longer maintained. A frozen instrument belongs in the archive, with its last update visible. [10]

# The feeding bill

[CONCEPT: Operating prices are the charges for using an AI system. A fixed basket of billed input and output makes published text-processing rates easier to compare over time. It measures the price of that quantity, while usefulness and the cost of completing a real job require separate checks.]

## Operating prices / one fixed basket of text

[STATS:prices]

Cheap output and good value are different questions. A recurring price basket makes the first easier to follow, while leaving room to investigate the second.

[FIGURE:price_basket]

| Standard rates / per million tokens | Input | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Claude Haiku 5.5** | $0.10 | $0.50 |
| **GPT-6.1 Terra** | $1.00 | $5.00 |

A **token** is a piece of text used for billing. The basket contains **one million billed input tokens and 250,000 billed output tokens**, spread across prompts no larger than 100,000 tokens. At the published standard rates, it costs **$0.225** with Haiku and **$2.250** with Terra. These are calculated charges, not bills from an experiment. Rates checked October 28. [4], [7]

**What the basket leaves out:** caching and batch discounts, tools, taxes, retries and human checking. The same volume can produce different quality, and some systems use additional billed reasoning tokens. The basket fixes the total billed quantity; it does not estimate a complete job's cost.

**Quarterly rule:** follow these exact models and pricing tiers. Add new entrants alongside them; mark retired or unavailable models as unavailable, never as free. A second dated capture creates a price change. Today's two-model comparison is an initial cohort, not a market-wide price index.

The companion question is **cost per usable result**. That belongs beside quality, time and human review in a matched task study. Different experiments cannot be stitched into a cheap-looking trend merely because they all report dollars. [40]

# The metabolic bill

[CONCEPT: Running AI requires computing equipment and electricity. To understand its resource demands, we must distinguish measured or estimated consumption from forecasts, and AI workloads from other computing. Global data-centre electricity totals include both AI and non-AI activity.]

[STATS:energy]

[FIGURE:energy_outlook]

| Regular track | How it will be carried forward |
| --- | --- |
| **Annual consumption** | Preserve the year and source edition. Carry the latest estimate until a new one is published. |
| **Forecast revisions** | Compare the same target year and scenario; retain earlier forecasts in the archive. |

**Numbers still to earn a permanent place:** AI-caused job-loss totals, sustained earnings gains, customer retention and model survival rates. These need consistent definitions and adequate coverage.

**Next-quarter comparison:** preserve source dates, disclose corrections and distinguish new observations from revisions. Missing data never become zeros or invented trends.

# October 6

[CONCEPT: AI-assisted research can increase the volume of proposed mathematical results. Turning those proposals into reliable knowledge still requires checking, explanation and follow-up. The central issue is how a research community handles a larger supply of claims without treating quantity as proof of value.]

## When mathematical papers fall like rain

[STATS:math-catalogue]

[PLATE:mathematical-rain:142]

One remarkable paper can keep a research community busy. A collection numbering in the hundreds changes the scale of the encounter.

On October 6, OpenAI released mathematical work produced by an internal AI system. The public collection includes proposed results at different stages of checking. At our October 27 visit, its catalogue listed **721 manuscripts in 373 related groups** and described the material as research output still needing scrutiny and refinement. These are catalogue counts, not a tally of accepted breakthroughs. [1], [11]

Picture the Proofweaver suddenly filling a clearing with webs. Some may be strong, some may need repair, and some may contain a connection nobody has noticed before. The naturalist now has two jobs: inspect what has appeared, and ask how the clearing itself has changed.

The independent Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence stressed that releasing the material began the process of understanding it. It also argued that mathematicians must remain free to choose their own questions rather than become an aftercare service for problems chosen by AI companies. Its statement did not endorse all the results or speak for every mathematician. [13]

**Why it matters for AI's future:** the scarce resource may shift. If useful candidate discoveries become easier to produce, careful checking, good explanations, and the choice of worthwhile questions could become more valuable. That is our interpretation of the emerging pattern, not a measured forecast of the profession's future.

### Elsewhere in the clearing: Le Chonk

Mistral announced a preview of Large 4 and a plan to release downloadable model weights by the end of October. Weights are the learned settings needed to run a model. [2] Their release could let more people house and adapt the creature themselves, although equipment, skill, and release terms still determine who can actually do so.

The field question is whether independent descendants will establish new niches. The announcement alone does not supply those descendants.

## The office reef acquires hands

On October 8, Google Cloud announced a Gemini agent for work, describing connections to business systems, tools, and Workspace applications. [5] On October 13, Google announced Gemini 4 Argon's availability in the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers, alongside expanded ways to carry out tasks. [6]

# Beyond the Glasshouse

## The nursery problem

[STATS:learning]

[PLATE:learning-nursery:120]

The colony is eating better than ever. The young have stopped learning how to forage. These statements could both be true. That is the uncomfortable possibility raised by Dylan Davis's video about AI and expertise, and by the fuller passage supplied for this workshop. [47]

**Nature sometimes makes room for a lesson.** Wild meerkats give pups opportunities to handle prey. In another study, an experienced ant guided a follower toward food, with signals between them regulating the journey. [52], [53] Feeding another animal and helping it acquire a skill are different achievements. Our workplace analogy begins with that distinction.

If the Relay Octopus sorts every document and drafts every answer, the office may become wonderfully productive. # The borrowed metabolism

[CONCEPT: AI dependence arises when people or organizations rely on an outside system for an important function. Delegation can improve efficiency, while making interruption or loss of access more costly. Dependence and skill loss are different questions and should be tested separately.]

## When a helpful neighbour becomes essential

A microbe does not receive a medal for making everything itself. In the right surroundings, depending on a neighbour can pay.

The **Black Queen hypothesis** describes how microbes may lose costly functions when neighbouring organisms supply their benefits. Separate experiments with engineered bacteria found that partners exchanging essential nutrients could gain a growth advantage over self-sufficient cells under the tested conditions. [54], [55] Dependence can accompany success. The price is that the missing function must still be supplied somewhere.

[PLATE:borrowed-metabolism:166]

Our AI analogy is an organization that stops maintaining a capability because an assistant reliably supplies it. That might be sensible specialization. It might also leave the organization unable to recognize a bad result or cope when access changes. **Which outcome occurs is a question for observation.**

The analogy has a boundary: genes lost across microbial generations are different from human skills that are never acquired, become rusty, or are deliberately left to a tool. People can retrain and institutions can redesign work. Calling a skill "vestigial" would describe our metaphor, not establish a biological process.

**The human side has its own research history.** Bainbridge's 1983 analysis warned that automation could rely on experienced operators' knowledge while reducing opportunities to acquire or maintain it. Later AI research found that requiring people to think through a decision could reduce excessive reliance on incorrect advice, though it did not eliminate the problem. [56], [57]

The Relay Octopus's cord now carries a second question. We already ask what the AI depends on. We should also ask **what its users have come to depend on it for**. A partnership can be highly capable while one partner loses options.

# Keeping claws in reserve

[CONCEPT: Delegating work to AI changes which skills people exercise. A delegated skill may remain available, weaken through disuse or be replaced by another method. The practical question is whether people can still judge the output and recover when the assistant is unavailable.]

## A loss-of-function ledger for the human habitat

[STATS:learning]

[PLATE:claws-in-reserve:60]

Our gain-of-function pages follow what AI acquires. Alongside them, we can follow what people stop doing, what they still understand, and what they may need to recover. Delegation is not automatically a loss: the ability may remain intact, or a better way of working may replace it.

**Judgment needs practice too.** Asking someone to approve an answer is not proof that they can assess it. The learning and automation studies give us reasons to test that capacity, rather than assume a human signature supplies it. [44], [45], [56], [57] Nor do they show that beginners and experienced experts suddenly start from the same position.

For each workplace encounter, this proposed ledger would distinguish three choices. They are editorial questions, not a prescription to preserve every old skill.

| Treatment of a skill | Why keep or release it? | What could we observe? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep in regular use | Needed to understand the task, detect consequential errors, or choose worthwhile goals. | Explain a decision; check an unfamiliar example with planted mistakes. |
| Rehearse as a reserve | Needed occasionally when a system fails or conditions change. | Try a bounded exercise without the usual assistant; record help and recovery time. |
| Delegate with a fallback | Keeping full proficiency may cost more than it contributes in this setting. | Document the alternative person, tool or procedure, and test whether it works. |

**Proposed encounter: two drafts and a diagnosis.** On a safe practice task, have a learner attempt an answer before seeing AI's version. Compare both with an independently checked reference. This exercise is a design to test, not a proven training recipe.

**A new survival question:** does the partnership replenish the expertise it needs? A department can finish more work this quarter while its next generation learns less. It can also use AI to teach better. Our report should look for either outcome, and revisit the balance whenever the tools or the work change.

The future need not be a ladder on which humans endlessly retreat toward "strategy." Checking, choosing goals and teaching can also change. The interesting story is which useful abilities persist, where they reside, and who can still exercise them.

# Sources: a reader's research map

## Two sources should mean two lines of evidence

This issue's main concepts now draw on multiple research papers. Agreement can be partial; contrary findings belong in the story. A video, a company blog and a newspaper repeating the same experiment still count as one underlying study.

| Concept | Research to begin with | What the evidence supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More routes to capability | Scaling [34], [35]; reasoning [27], [36] | Resource allocation and problem-solving methods can matter. No single law guarantees unlimited progress. |
| Tools extend reach | ReAct [42]; Toolformer [43] | External tools can improve selected tasks. These experiments do not test October's products. |
| Teams have tradeoffs | Kim [37]; Cemri [38] | Coordination can help or hinder, depending on the task and system. |
| Reliability and cost matter | Kwa [39]; Kapoor [40] | A high score is incomplete evidence of practical usefulness. No general survival probability follows. |
| AI helps with AI research | RE-Bench [41]; PaperBench [51] | Some research tasks can be automated to a degree. A self-sustaining development cycle is a stronger claim. |
| Assistance changes learning | Bastani [44]; Shen [45]; Brynjolfsson [46] | Risks and benefits differ by task, tool design and use. Long-term professional outcomes remain open. |
| Mathematical contribution | AlphaGeometry [49]; Binder [16] | Different systems and partnerships can contribute. Correctness of each new result needs its own assessment. |

**How to read the notes.** Peer-reviewed means other researchers assessed the paper before publication; it does not make the findings final. A preprint is a public research manuscript whose review status is not established here. Company measurements remain company measurements, even when written as a paper.

The species, their anatomy and our speciation criteria are editorial devices. Future envelopes are forecasts. Neither is presented as an empirical conclusion established by the papers. Single-source reports of particular events remain explicitly attributed and cannot carry a broad conclusion on their own.

# Sources: routes to capability

## Research beneath the doors and the flock

[35] Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues. **Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models.** Research paper, 2022. Tests the balance between model size and training data under a computing budget. Paired with Kaplan [34]; different prescriptions are not proof of a universal growth law. https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556

[36] Charlie Snell, Jaehoon Lee, Kelvin Xu and Aviral Kumar. **Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters.** 2024 paper; conference version at ICLR 2025. Experiments show that the value of additional work before answering depends on the problem and strategy. This is not evidence that longer answers always mean better reasoning. https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314

[37] Yubin Kim and colleagues. **Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems.** Preprint first submitted December 2025; version 3, April 8, 2026. Controlled comparisons find gains and losses depending on coordination and task structure. [38] Mert Cemri and colleagues. **Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?** Research manuscript, version 3, October 26, 2025. Analyzes system-design, coordination and task-checking failures. [39] Thomas Kwa and colleagues. **Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks.** NeurIPS 2025; manuscript version 4, July 10, 2026. # Sources: tools and research assistants

## A useful component is not a complete autonomous scientist

[41] Hjalmar Wijk and colleagues. **RE-Bench: Evaluating frontier AI R&D capabilities of language model agents against human experts.** Research manuscript, November 2024; version 2, May 27, 2025. Compares agents and people on seven research-engineering environments. Relative performance depends on time budget. [42] Shunyu Yao and colleagues. **ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models.** ICLR 2023. Combines intermediate reasoning with actions and access to outside information. [43] Timo Schick and colleagues. **Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools.** Research paper, February 2023. Trains a model to choose and use tools including a calculator and information services. Training is part of this method: do not cite it as proof that every tool improvement leaves the model unchanged. https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761

[51] Giulio Starace and colleagues. **PaperBench: Evaluating AI's Ability to Replicate AI Research.** OpenAI-led research manuscript, April 2025, version 3. Evaluates attempts to reproduce twenty papers, grading component tasks. Partial replication is not novel discovery or autonomous creation of a successor model. **Date discipline.** These studies provide background for questions raised in Q4. Their older model scores are not current capability ceilings. Neither their achievements nor their limitations determine when fully autonomous research will arrive. Our E-03 envelope concerns a future public demonstration and remains a provisional editorial forecast.

# Sources: the nursery of expertise

## Today's output and tomorrow's competence

[44] Hamsa Bastani and colleagues. **Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics.** PNAS, June 25, 2025. Peer-reviewed field experiment in one Turkish high school, with nearly a thousand students. Unrestricted assistance improved assisted practice but reduced subsequent unaided performance; a teaching-oriented design largely mitigated that loss. Short educational setting, not a career-length study. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

An August 2025 correction concerns an author's affiliation, not the results. Correction checked: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122

[45] Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin. **How AI Impacts Skill Formation.** Anthropic research preprint, January 28, 2026; version 2, February 1. Randomized experiments with developers learning an unfamiliar programming library found weaker understanding with AI assistance on average. The study measured learning over about an hour, not a career. Some interaction patterns involving active thinking preserved learning; these patterns are exploratory observations, not separately randomized teaching interventions. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245v2

[46] Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond. **Generative AI at Work.** Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), 889-942, 2025. Peer-reviewed study of an assistant's staggered introduction among 5,172 support agents. Benefits were strongest among less experienced and lower-performing workers; the authors also report evidence consistent with learning. One workplace, a different task and study design from [44]-[45]; this is a useful counterweight, not a direct replication. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044

[47] Dylan Davis. **AI Just Broke the Way People Become Experts.** YouTube commentary, October 21, 2026, 12:33. Title, creator, date and duration checked on the video page. The fuller user-supplied passage received October 27 develops the apprenticeship and delegation argument; it is retained with the research notes and has not been checked word-for-word against the audio. Neither is academic corroboration. This is separate from the 15:14 scaling transcript [26]. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmCPS7h08pY

**What remains open.** These studies support a conditional account of learning. They do not demonstrate society-wide loss of expertise, prove every AI tutor beneficial, or forecast employment. Workplaces' responses and longer-term outcomes remain reporting questions.

# Sources: teaching and dependence

## Biological roots of the new field notes

[52] Alex Thornton and Katherine McAuliffe. **Teaching in wild meerkats.** Science 313, 227-229, 2006. Observational and experimental evidence of adults facilitating pups' acquisition of prey-handling skills. Supports the biological teaching example, not a claim that human workplaces or all animals learn in the same way. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1128727

[53] Nigel R. Franks and Tom Richardson. **Teaching in tandem-running ants.** Nature 439, 153, 2006. The leader and follower exchange signals during travel to food. A separate species and study supporting the broader point that assistance can transmit useful skills or information. The follower is not a juvenile: the analogy concerns teaching, not an ant nursery. https://doi.org/10.1038/439153a

[54] J. Jeffrey Morris, Richard E. Lenski and Erik R. Zinser. **The Black Queen Hypothesis: Evolution of Dependencies through Adaptive Gene Loss.** mBio 3, e00036-12, 2012. Hypothesis article explaining how loss of costly functions may be favoured when their benefits remain available from neighbours. A proposed evolutionary explanation, not a direct study of human skill loss. https://doi.org/10.1128/mBio.00036-12

[55] Samay Pande and colleagues. **Fitness and stability of obligate cross-feeding interactions that emerge upon gene loss in bacteria.** The ISME Journal 8, 953-962, 2014; published online November 28, 2013. Experiments used engineered E. coli partners that exchanged amino acids. Some partnerships gained a growth advantage in the tested setting. This is distinct experimental evidence for benefits of metabolic dependence, not proof of every mechanism or prediction in [54]. https://doi.org/10.1038/ismej.2013.211

**What crosses the analogy.** Assistance can transmit a capability; an outside supplier can also make self-sufficiency less valuable. What does not automatically cross is the mechanism, timescale, reversibility or outcome. Animal teaching, microbial gene loss, individual learning and organizational choices remain different processes.

**Two sets of evidence.** The biological comparisons use [52]-[55]. Claims about human learning draw separately on [44]-[46], with automation research [56]-[57]. The nursery and borrowed-metabolism labels are this report's editorial interpretations. These older studies are background to the October discussion, not new Q4 discoveries.

# Sources: inheritance and technological change

## Several ways for an earlier achievement to travel

[64] Edward J. Hu and colleagues. **LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models.** International Conference on Learning Representations, 2022; manuscript first posted in 2021. Keeps the underlying model's learned settings fixed while training a smaller set of added adjustments for particular tasks. A concrete route from a shared base to specialized variants; it does not establish reproductive isolation or new biological-style species. https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685

[65] Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals and Jeff Dean. **Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network.** Workshop paper, public manuscript March 2015. Trains a model using information supplied by another model or ensemble, demonstrating transfer through teaching outputs rather than simple copying of all learned settings. Different mechanism and team from [64]; neither paper measures an industry-wide inheritance rate. https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531

[66] W. Brian Arthur and Wolfgang Polak. **The evolution of technology within a simple computer model.** Complexity 11(5), 23-31, 2006. A computer experiment constructs logic circuits from earlier components and retains useful combinations. Supports a mechanism of cumulative recombination and dependence on available building blocks. It is a simplified technology model, not an empirical study of AI firms. https://doi.org/10.1002/cplx.20130

Earlier author-hosted working-paper version, dated December 21, 2004: https://sites.santafe.edu/~wbarthur/Papers/AP-Complexity.pdf

**Separate the routes.** Retained settings [64], teaching from outputs [65], and component recombination [66] are complementary examples, not three replications of one experiment. ReAct [42] and Toolformer [43] provide additional evidence about composing models with tools. The metaphor must preserve those differences.

**No hidden ancestry claims.** We do not infer that a particular commercial model is descended from another because their answers look alike. Shared components, copied methods and independently similar solutions require different evidence. Model names and marketing families cannot establish ancestry by themselves.
